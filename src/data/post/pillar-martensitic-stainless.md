# Martensitic Stainless Steel for Cutting Tools

Martensitic stainless steels are the workhorse corrosion-resistant grades for industrial cutting tools that must survive water, food contact, mild chemicals, or atmospheric corrosion. The defining feature is a martensitic matrix that can be hardened by heat treatment to HRC 40–60, with sufficient chromium (≥12 %) to provide passivation in mildly corrosive environments. Unlike austenitic 300-series stainless (304, 316), martensitic stainless grades are magnetic and can be hardened — making them suitable for knife blades, bearings, surgical instruments and food-processing equipment.

For industrial cutting tools, the martensitic stainless family is the right choice when corrosion resistance matters as much as wear life — food-contact slitter blades, meat-bone granulator rotors, marine cutting tools, and pump / valve components. The five grades covered in this guide — AISI 420, 440A, 440B, 440C and 17-4 PH — span the range from basic food-grade stainless to the highest-hardness corrosion-resistant grade available.

Selection follows three steps: identify the corrosion environment (food-grade vs mild chemicals vs marine), identify the required hardness (cutting edge needs HRC 50+), and match the grade to the application. 420 is the basic entry grade; the 440 family is the workhorse for industrial blades; 17-4 PH fills the niche where high strength matters more than wear life.

For per-grade chemistry, heat treatment and cross-reference tables, see the [AISI 420 reference entry](/blog/materials-encyclopedia/420/), [440A entry](/blog/materials-encyclopedia/440a/), [440B entry](/blog/materials-encyclopedia/440b/), [440C entry](/blog/materials-encyclopedia/440c/), and [17-4 PH entry](/blog/materials-encyclopedia/17-4ph/).

## 1. What is martensitic stainless steel?

Martensitic stainless steel is one of the five families of stainless steel — the others being austenitic (300-series), ferritic (430), duplex (2205) and precipitation-hardening (17-4 PH, 15-5 PH). The defining feature is a body-centred tetragonal (BCT) martensite matrix after heat treatment, which is hardenable. Martensitic stainless grades contain 11.5–18 % Cr and 0.1–1.1 % C; the chromium provides passivation, the carbon enables hardening.

The martensitic stainless family used for cutting tools covers:
- **420 family** — basic martensitic, HRC 48–54, food-grade toughness
- **440 family** — higher-carbon martensitic, HRC 54–60, workhorse for industrial blades
- **17-4 PH** — precipitation-hardening martensitic, HRC 28–44, high strength (1,300 MPa UTS)

The 440 family is further subdivided:
- **440A** (UNS S44002) — lowest carbon, optimised for corrosion over hardness
- **440B** (UNS S44003) — mid carbon, balanced
## 21. Real-world case: extending 440C slitter blade life 3×

A paper-converting line was experiencing 440C slitter blade wear-out every 14 days at standard specification (HRC 60, no coating, 5 µm edge hone). Annual blade replacement cost: ~$48,000. Failure mode: abrasive wear on the cutting edge with mild edge rounding (no chipping).

The optimization sequence:

1. **Drop hardness to HRC 56–58**: 2 HRC drop buys 20 % chip resistance for 15 % wear-life penalty. Net win for this application.

2. **Increase edge hone to 15 µm**: 3× the hone width, vastly improves chip resistance for a small wear-life cost.

3. **Apply PVD AlCrN coating (3 µm)**: coating hardness ~3200 HV, reduces friction, extends wear life 2–3× on the abrasive paper substrate.

4. **Verify passivation after coating**: the coating process can damage the passive film on stainless substrate. ASTM A967 re-passivation after coating restores the corrosion margin.

5. **Implement wear-tracking protocol**: measure edge radius every 3 days, replace at 80 % of failure threshold rather than 100 %. Reduces unscheduled downtime.

**Result**: blade service life extended from 14 to 42 days (3×). Annual cost: $48,000 / 3 = $16,000. Total savings: $32,000/year. ROI on the optimisation effort: 100×.

The combination of substrate optimization + edge geometry + coating + post-coating passivation + monitoring is what makes a blade specification durable. Any one optimisation alone gives modest gains; the combined approach multiplies the gains.
## 20. Food-contact regulatory framework

Martensitic stainless blades used in food processing must comply with regional food-safety regulations. The key regulatory frameworks:

**FDA 21 CFR (US)**: food-contact surfaces must be "food-grade" — typically meaning austenitic 300-series stainless or martensitic 400-series that passes the FDA extractables test (heavy metals extraction into food simulants). 420 and 440C are both FDA-compliant for food contact when properly passivated. 17-4 PH requires separate testing due to the Cu content.

**EU Regulation 1935/2004**: food-contact materials must not transfer constituents to food in quantities endangering health, changing food composition, deteriorating organoleptic properties. 420 / 440C / 17-4 PH all pass for typical food applications when the Cr-Ni-Cu extraction levels are within limits.

**GB 4806.1 (China)**: similar to EU 1935/2004 with specific extractables limits for stainless steel food contact.

**NSF / ANSI 51 (food equipment materials)**: standard for food equipment materials in North America. Specifies maximum lead content and other heavy-metal limits. Martensitic stainless generally meets NSF 51.

For a food-contact slitter blade in the EU market, the documentation trail should include:
- Mill certificate with full chemistry
- Passivation certificate per ASTM A967
- Surface roughness measurement (typically Ra ≤0.8 µm)
- Optional: biocompatibility test per ISO 10993 (for medical / pharmaceutical applications)
- Optional: extractables test per FDA 21 CFR or EU 1935/2004

The compliance cost adds ~$200–500 per shipment — typically amortised over the production volume. For a high-volume food-contact slitter line, the compliance cost is negligible; for low-volume custom blades, it can be significant.

For pharmaceutical applications (tablet cutting, pill splitting, surgical instruments), the additional standards are:
- **USP Class VI** (US Pharmacopoeia) — biological reactivity testing
- **ISO 10993** — biocompatibility
- **ASTM F899** — standard specification for stainless steel surgical instruments

These standards apply to the final surface finish, not the bulk material. The passivation and electropolishing steps above satisfy most compliance requirements; the additional cost is documentation and testing rather than additional processing.
## 17. Edge geometry and hone for stainless

Martensitic stainless edge geometry follows the same principles as tool steel (see the [cold-work pillar §17](/blog/selection-guide/pillar-cold-work-tool-steel/) for full discussion) but with two important differences:

**Avoid over-grinding burns**: 440C is more sensitive to grinding burns than D2 because the lower thermal conductivity (~25 W/m·K vs D2 ~25 W/m·K similar, but the higher Cr content makes it more susceptible to oxidation discoloration at lower temperatures). Use sharp, dressed grinding wheels with abundant coolant. A blue or straw-coloured burn on the edge indicates over-tempering — the blade will crack in service.

**Honematching rule of thumb for stainless**: a 5 µm edge hone on 440C at HRC 60 will chip on a wet food line (where tramp ice / bone fragments impact the edge). Move to:
- 440C at HRC 56 with 15 µm hone — for wet food lines with impact
- 17-4 PH at HRC 40 (H900) with 10 µm hone — for structural blades where strength matters

**Micro-bevel for slitter blades**: 30° secondary bevel at 0.05–0.10 mm width dramatically improves chipping resistance without sacrificing cut quality. This is standard for all premium food-contact slitter blades.

For food-contact blades, the edge radius specification is typically 5–15 µm (paper / film), 15–25 µm (food products), or 25–50 µm (meat-bone / high-impact).

## 18. Worked example: selecting stainless for a meat-bone granulator

A meat-bone granulator rotor blade needs:
- Corrosion resistance to blood, salt brine and cleaning chemicals (CIP wash)
- Wear life on bone (abrasive Ca₃(PO₄)₂)
- High-impact resistance (granulator rotors see 100+ G shock loads per revolution)
- 6+ months service life before re-sharpening

**Grade selection process**:

1. Corrosion environment: blood + salt + CIP wash. 440C adequate; 17-4 PH better for marine / brine.
2. Hardness requirement: granulator rotors work best at HRC 58–60 for wear life.
3. Toughness requirement: high impact — 440C at HRC 60 chips frequently; 440C at HRC 56–58 better.
4. Cost: ~5,000 rotor blades per set × $15 per blade (440C) = $75,000 per set. 17-4 PH at $25 per blade = $125,000 — 67 % premium.

**Recommendation**: 440C at HRC 56–58 with a 20–30 µm hone and PVD AlCrN coating. The lower hardness + hone + coating combination extends service life 2–3× over uncoated 440C at HRC 60, at lower cost than 17-4 PH.

Verify with a 1-week wear test in the actual production environment before committing to full production volumes. The granulator wear-test protocol: install 3 candidate blades, run 1 week, measure edge wear (micrometer) and document failure mode (chipping, wear, deformation).

## 15. Specialty applications

Beyond food-contact slitter blades, martensitic stainless grades are widely used in:

**Bearings and races**: 440C is the standard for corrosion-resistant ball bearings and roller bearings — used in food-processing machinery, marine equipment, medical devices, and aerospace actuators. Bearings require tight dimensional stability (hence the sub-zero treatment standard for 440C bearings) and high wear life at moderate contact stress.

**Surgical and dental instruments**: 420 and 440A are standard for surgical scalpels, scissors, dental burs, and orthodontic pliers. The combination of corrosion resistance, hardness for edge retention, and ability to be repeatedly sterilised (autoclaving at 134 °C) makes 420/440A the right choice.

**Pump and valve components**: 420 is widely used for pump shafts, valve stems, and valve seats where corrosion resistance is needed but the cutting duty is light. 17-4 PH H1150 is used for high-pressure valve bodies where strength matters more than hardness.

**Knife industry**: 440C and 17-4 PH are the standard for premium kitchen knives and outdoor knives. 440C offers edge retention comparable to D2 tool steel with the bonus of corrosion resistance. 17-4 PH offers high strength for tactical and survival knives.

**Marine cutting tools**: 440C is the standard for fishing-knife blades, marine rope cutters, and underwater cutting tools. The 16–18 % Cr provides resistance to seawater corrosion — although 440C is not immune to seawater (316L austenitic is preferred for permanent submersion).

**Aerospace**: 17-4 PH H900 is standard for aerospace actuator components, structural blades, and high-strength fasteners. The combination of 1,300 MPa UTS with corrosion resistance makes it the right choice for critical aerospace applications.

## 13. Corrosion testing and standards

Martensitic stainless corrosion resistance is verified by standardised tests. The common tests, in order of severity:

**ASTM A967 passivation** — chemical passivation in nitric acid (20–50 % v/v) or citric acid (4–10 % w/w) for 30 minutes minimum at room temperature. Removes free iron from the surface and enhances the Cr₂O₃ passive film. Standard test for all food-contact and medical-grade martensitic stainless. Specified in ASTM A967.

**ASTM A380** — cleaning, descaling and passivation of stainless steel parts. Defines the surface preparation, acid pickling, and passivation procedure. Used in conjunction with A967.

**ASTM B117 salt spray** — exposure to 5 % NaCl fog at 35 °C. The time to first rust is the rating. For 440C, expect 100–500 hours before first rust (depending on surface finish and passivation). For 420, expect 50–200 hours. For 17-4 PH H900, 200–1000 hours.

**ASTM G48 pitting corrosion** — exposure to 6 % FeCl₃ solution for 72 hours. The critical pitting temperature (CPT) is the rating. For 440C, CPT ~20–30 °C. For 17-4 PH H1150 (low Cu, low-aged condition), CPT ~40 °C. Above CPT, pitting initiates and propagates rapidly.

**ISO 4538** — electrochemical critical pitting temperature test. More accurate than ASTM G48 but requires electrochemical setup.

For food-contact applications, the FDA / USDA standard is ASTM A967 passivation plus electropolishing for high-purity applications. For medical devices (ISO 13485), the additional requirement is biocompatibility testing (ISO 10993) on the final surface finish.

## 14. Passivation and electropolishing

The corrosion resistance of martensitic stainless depends on the integrity of the Cr₂O₃ passive film. Two post-processing treatments significantly enhance corrosion performance:

**Chemical passivation (ASTM A967)**: immersion in nitric or citric acid removes free iron from the surface and enhances the passive film. Standard for all food-contact and medical-grade blades. Cost: ~$0.50–2.00 per blade depending on size. Effect: 2–4× improvement in salt-spray performance.

**Electropolishing**: electrochemical removal of surface material in a phosphoric-sulfuric acid bath. Smooths the surface to ~Ra 0.1–0.4 µm (vs ~Ra 0.8–1.6 µm for mechanical polishing) and enhances the Cr-to-Fe ratio at the surface. Cost: ~$5–20 per blade. Effect: 4–10× improvement in corrosion resistance; also improves cleanability (important for food-contact and pharmaceutical).

**Mechanical surface finish**: the surface finish before passivation matters. A ground surface (Ra 0.4–0.8 µm) has more nucleation sites for the passive film than a rough surface. A polished surface (Ra 0.1–0.4 µm) is even better. Standard spec for food-contact blades: ground to Ra 0.4 µm, then passivated.

For maximum corrosion resistance on food-contact or pharmaceutical blades, the sequence is:
1. Grind to Ra 0.4–0.8 µm
2. Electropolish to Ra 0.1–0.4 µm
3. Passivate per ASTM A967 (citric acid preferred for medical)
4. Final rinse with deionised water
5. Dry and package in clean-room environment

This sequence gives 4–10× the corrosion life of a simple ground-and-passivated blade.

## 7. Heat treatment

Martensitic stainless heat treatment has four stages: austenitising, quenching, sub-zero (optional), and tempering. The presence of chromium changes the metallurgy versus plain carbon steel — austenitising temperatures are higher, and care must be taken to avoid sensitisation (chromium-carbide precipitation in the 400–500 °C range that reduces corrosion resistance).

**Austenitising** for 420 / 440A-B-C: 980–1050 °C for 30 minutes per inch of section. Hold time is critical — too short and primary carbides are not fully dissolved (low hardness); too long and grain growth occurs (low toughness). For 17-4 PH: solution anneal at 1040 °C for 30 minutes per inch, then air cool.

**Quenching** for 420 / 440A-B-C: oil quench (50–80 °C oil) for thin sections, air cool or positive-pressure gas quench for heavy sections. Water quench is not recommended — distortion and cracking risk is too high. For 17-4 PH: air cool from solution anneal temperature.

**Sub-zero treatment** (cooling to −80 °C in liquid nitrogen or dry-ice alcohol after quenching) transforms most retained austenite to martensite. Recommended for 440C when dimensional stability is critical (bearings, gauges). Sub-zero should be done within 1 hour of quench and before the first temper.

**Tempering** for 420 / 440A-B-C: 150–200 °C for 2 hours, single or double temper. Higher tempering temperatures (above 400 °C) cause sensitisation — chromium-carbide precipitation at grain boundaries, which reduces corrosion resistance. **For food-contact or corrosive applications, do not temper above 200 °C** — the resulting chromium-carbide precipitation leaves the steel susceptible to intergranular corrosion. For 17-4 PH: aging at 480–620 °C as described in §5.

**Sensitisation caution**: the 400–500 °C temper range is a "sensitisation zone" for martensitic stainless — chromium carbide precipitates at grain boundaries, depleting the surrounding matrix of free chromium. The result is intergranular corrosion susceptibility, even though the bulk hardness is unaffected. Standard practice: temper below 200 °C for corrosive applications. For non-corrosive applications (bearings, gauges), the H900 / H1025 aging of 17-4 PH can use higher temperatures because the strengthening mechanism is different (Cu precipitation, not Cr carbide).

## 8. Common failure modes

The three most common martensitic stainless blade failures in production:

**1. Pitting corrosion from chloride attack.** A food-contact slitter blade shows small dark pits after 2 weeks of service in salt-brine environment. Visual tell: small dark spots on the surface, often around sharp edges or where the passive film was damaged. Fix: upgrade from 420 to 440C (more Cr), or upgrade to 17-4 PH (different strengthening mechanism, more corrosion-resistant). For the affected blade, electropolish to remove the pits and re-passivate.

**2. Edge chipping from low toughness.** A 440C blade at HRC 60 chatters on a recycled-paper line. Visual tell: edge fragments missing, often 0.5–2 mm. Fix: drop hardness 2 HRC to HRC 58, or move to 440A (lower hardness, higher toughness), or add 10–20 µm hone to the edge.

**3. Sensitisation from incorrect tempering.** A 440C blade tempered at 450 °C shows intergranular corrosion in service. Visual tell: grain-boundary cracks under magnification, especially after exposure to corrosive media. Fix: re-temper at 150–200 °C (below the sensitisation zone), or replace with a new blade tempered to specification.

A useful diagnostic is to inspect the failed blade at 10–30× magnification. Pitting → corrosion problem (upgrade grade or improve passivation); chipping → impact problem (drop hardness or hone edge); intergranular cracks → sensitisation problem (correct tempering).
## 5. 17-4 PH precipitation-hardening stainless

**17-4 PH (UNS S17400, AISI 630, DIN 1.4542)** is a precipitation-hardening martensitic stainless — 0.07 % C max, 15.0–17.5 % Cr, 3.0–5.0 % Ni, 3.0–5.0 % Cu, 0.15–0.45 % Nb. The low carbon means it cannot be hardened by quenching in the same way as the 420/440 family. Instead, 17-4 PH is solution annealed and then aged at 480–620 °C for 1–4 h to precipitate Cu-rich phases that raise the strength.

The achievable hardness depends on the aging temperature:
- **H900** (age at 480 °C, 1 h) — HRC 40–44, UTS 1,300 MPa
- **H1025** (age at 550 °C, 4 h) — HRC 36–40, UTS 1,150 MPa
- **H1150** (age at 620 °C, 4 h) — HRC 28–32, UTS 1,000 MPa

The H900 condition gives the highest hardness but lowest toughness; H1150 gives the best toughness. For cutting tools, H900 to H1025 is typical.

17-4 PH is specified where high strength (1,300 MPa UTS) matters more than wear life — aerospace structural blades, die-casting dies, plastic-injection moulds, food-processing parts requiring high strength + corrosion resistance. For pure cutting-edge wear life, the 440C family outperforms 17-4 PH at the same corrosion class — the precipitation hardening in 17-4 PH does not produce hard M₂₃C₆ carbides; the matrix itself is harder, but the wear life is lower than 440C.

17-4 PH selection rule: use 17-4 PH when the application requires high structural strength (1,000–1,300 MPa UTS) and corrosion resistance, but the cutting duty is moderate. Do not use 17-4 PH for heavy abrasive cutting — 440C gives 2–3× the wear life at the same corrosion class.

## 6. Selection methodology

A pragmatic selection methodology for martensitic stainless cutting tools:

1. **Identify the corrosion environment**:
   - Food contact (occasional water, salt, mild organic acids): 420 / 440A / 440C / 17-4 PH all adequate
   - Mild chemicals (dilute acids, bleach, detergents): 440C minimum; 17-4 PH preferred
   - Marine / salt spray: 440C or 17-4 PH; avoid 420 (lower Cr)
   - High-purity pharmaceutical / semiconductor: 440C or electropolished 440C

2. **Identify the required hardness**:
   - Soft cutting (slicing, dicing, portioning): HRC 48–54 → 420 adequate
   - Medium cutting (slitting, dicing, food processing): HRC 54–58 → 440A or 440B
   - Heavy industrial cutting (slitter blades, granulator rotors): HRC 58–60 → 440C

3. **Check toughness requirements**:
   - Thin sections (<2 mm), impact loading: 440A or 17-4 PH
   - Standard sections (2–10 mm): 440B or 440C
   - Heavy sections (>10 mm), static loading: 440C

4. **Verify corrosion-resistance**:
   - ASTM A967 passivation (nitric or citric acid) is standard for food-contact blades. Electropolishing improves corrosion resistance by 2–4× for high-purity applications.

5. **Cost-vs-performance trade-off**: 420 is the cheapest at ~$5–10/kg finished. 440C is ~$10–15/kg. 17-4 PH is ~$15–25/kg. The price ladder reflects the higher alloy content and more complex heat treatment.

6. **Match the grade to the application**:
   - Food slicing / dicing blades → 420 (basic) or 440A (premium)
   - Food slitter blades → 440C
   - Meat-bone granulator rotors → 440C (or 17-4 PH for high-strength rotors)
   - Surgical instruments → 420 / 440A
   - Pump shafts / valve parts → 420
   - Ball bearings / roller bearings → 440C
   - Aerospace structural blades → 17-4 PH
   - Die-casting dies → 17-4 PH (or H11/H13 hot-work, see [hot-work pillar](/blog/selection-guide/pillar-hot-work-tool-steel/))
## 3. The 420 family

**AISI 420 (UNS S42000, DIN 1.4021)** is the basic entry-grade martensitic stainless — 0.15 % C min, 12.0–14.0 % Cr. Hardened to HRC 48–54, with high corrosion resistance and good toughness. 420 is the default grade for food-processing blades, surgical instruments, pump shafts, valve parts and low-stress cutlery.

420 is often the right choice when the application requires food-grade corrosion resistance but the cutting duty is light — slicing, dicing, portioning. For heavier industrial blade duty (slitting film with mineral fillers, cutting abrasive polymers), 420 wears too quickly; the 440 family is required.

**Variants**: 420 has two common variants — 420HC (Bohler K720) at 0.40 % C for cutlery, and 420F (UNS S42020) with added sulfur for free-machining. For industrial cutting tools, the standard 420 is preferred; 420F should be avoided in cutting edges because the sulfide inclusions reduce toughness.

420 selection rule: use 420 for food-grade applications where impact loading dominates and wear life is not the bottleneck. Do not use 420 for slitter blades on abrasive film, granulator rotors on filled polymer, or any application where the cutting edge sees heavy abrasive wear — the 440 family gives 3–10× the wear life at the same corrosion class.

## 4. The 440 family

The 440 family (440A, 440B, 440C) is the workhorse martensitic stainless for industrial cutting tools. With 16–18 % Cr and 0.6–1.1 % C, the 440 family achieves higher hardness than 420 while retaining sufficient free chromium for passivation. The M₂₃C₆ primary carbides in 440C are the largest in the martensitic stainless family and contribute to its high wear resistance.

**AISI 440A (UNS S44002, DIN 1.4109)** is the lowest-carbon 440-grade — 0.6–0.75 % C, 16–18 % Cr. Hardened to HRC 54–56. 440A is the most corrosion-resistant 440-grade because more of its chromium is free for passivation. Used where 440B/440C hardness is unnecessary but the corrosion resistance of the 440 family is required: food-contact slitter blades, pump shafts, lower-end cutlery, food-processing blades that handle salt brine.

**AISI 440B (UNS S44003, DIN 1.4112)** is the mid-carbon 440-grade — 0.75–0.95 % C, 16–18 % Cr. Hardened to HRC 56–58. 440B is the balance between 440A (corrosion) and 440C (hardness), used in industrial knives and bearings where 440A lacks wear life but 440C is over-specified for toughness.

**AISI 440C (UNS S44004, EN 1.4125)** is the highest-carbon 440-grade — 0.95–1.20 % C, 16–18 % Cr. Hardened to HRC 58–60. 440C has the highest strength, hardness and wear resistance of the 440-series. The large M₂₃C₆ primary carbides (~17 % by volume) provide wear life comparable to low-alloy tool steels while retaining the corrosion resistance of stainless. 440C is the workhorse grade for industrial cutting tools that need both wear life and corrosion resistance — food-contact slitter blades, meat-bone granulator rotors, surgical instruments, food-processing cutting tools, marine cutting tools, and ball bearings / roller bearings.

440 family selection rule: start with 440C for industrial cutting applications. Drop to 440B if the cutting duty is lighter and you want extra corrosion margin. Drop to 440A only if the application is primarily corrosion-driven (e.g. food-contact blades that don't see heavy abrasion). The wear-life penalty going from 440C to 440B is ~30 %; from 440B to 440A is another ~30 %.

**International equivalents**: 440C is equivalent to JIS SUS440C, DIN 1.4125, GB 11Cr17, and (close but not identical) Chinese 9Cr18MoV. The Mo-bearing 9Cr18MoV has slightly higher toughness at the cost of slightly lower hardness; see the [9Cr18MoV vs 440C comparison](/blog/material-comparison/9cr18mov-vs-440c/) for details.
- **440C** (UNS S44004) — highest carbon, the workhorse for industrial cutting tools

The trade-off in the 440 family is between corrosion resistance and hardness. More carbon means more carbides (M₂₃C₆ for 440-series) which raise hardness but reduce the chromium available for passivation. 440A is the most corrosion-resistant; 440C is the hardest.

## 2. Chemistry and passivation

The chemistry of martensitic stainless is dominated by three elements: chromium, carbon and (for some grades) nickel or molybdenum.

**Chromium** provides the passivation layer that makes the steel "stainless". A minimum of 10.5 % Cr is needed for atmospheric corrosion resistance; 12–18 % is the range for industrial martensitic stainless. The chromium forms a Cr₂O₃ passive film ~2 nm thick that self-repairs in the presence of oxygen. Damage to this film (by abrasion, impact, or chemical attack) is the rate-limiting step in corrosion — keep the film intact and the steel is corrosion-resistant.

**Carbon** drives the hardening response through martensite formation. At 0.15 % C (420), the achievable hardness is HRC 48–54. At 1.0 % C (440C), HRC 58–60. The trade-off with chromium is direct: chromium tied up in M₂₃C₆ carbides is not available for passivation. 440C with 1.0 % C has ~17 % of its chromium tied up in carbides; the free chromium available for passivation is closer to 13.5 %, still above the 10.5 % threshold but with reduced corrosion margin compared to 440A.

**Molybdenum** (in some grades, especially higher-end 440A variants) improves pitting resistance in chloride-rich environments — relevant for food-contact (salt) and marine applications.

**Nickel** is present in small amounts (≤0.75 %) in 420 / 440A-B-C as a residual from steelmaking. In 17-4 PH, nickel is a major alloying element (3.0–5.0 %) because the precipitation-hardening reaction requires Ni to form Cu-rich precipitates.

The carbon-vs-chromium trade-off is the single most important chemistry decision for martensitic stainless. More carbon gives more hardness (good for cutting edges) but reduces free chromium (bad for corrosion resistance). The 440 family strikes different balances: 440A keeps carbon low for corrosion, 440C raises carbon for cutting performance, 440B is the middle ground.

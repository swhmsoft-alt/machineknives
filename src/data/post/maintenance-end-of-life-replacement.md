A knife that is past its design life is a chip waiting to happen. Retiring at the right time prevents the most expensive failure modes (gross fracture, decarburised surface, geometry drift past spec) and frees up re-grind capacity to focus on the knives still in the rotation.

> **One-line summary:** *Retire when any one of four criteria is true: dimension below minimum spec, hardness below HRC 56, re-grind count above 25, or visible damage. Tag the retired knife red, segregate it, and re-purpose it for non-production use.*

---

## The four retirement criteria

1. **Dimension below minimum spec.** OD, ID, or thickness has dropped below the minimum specified on the drawing. For a 250 mm OD × 25 mm × 3 mm slitter, retirement thresholds: OD 240 mm, thickness 2.0 mm (after ~30 re-grinds).
2. **Hardness below HRC 56.** The 5-point file test fails. Either decarburisation has crept in, or the heat-treat has over-tempered. The knife is structurally unsafe.
3. **Re-grind count above 25.** Most precision knives are designed for 20–30 re-grinds. Beyond 25, the geometry is unrecoverable and heat-treat uniformity drifts.
4. **Visible damage.** Chips at the edge (cannot be re-ground), cracks in the body, or gross deformation (bent, warped, dented).

A knife that fails **any one** of the four is retired.

---

## The retirement protocol

When a knife is retired:

1. **Stamp the knife with a red tag.** "RETIRED — DO NOT USE IN PRODUCTION". Date and operator.
2. **Measure and log.** Record the final dimensions, hardness, and re-grind count.
3. **Move to the retired knife rack.** A separate, clearly-labelled storage area.
4. **Decide re-purpose or scrap.** A retired knife is rarely truly scrap.
5. **Update the inventory log.** Remove from in-service count; add to retired count.

The 5 minutes of retirement protocol saves the 1 hour of line troubleshooting if a retired knife is accidentally re-introduced.

---

## Re-purposing retired knives

A retired knife has lost production value but may have other uses:

- **Scrap chopper or anvil backup.** A retired D2 slitter is a fine anvil for hand-cutting.
- **Bench reference.** A retired knife with documented dimensions is a calibration reference.
- **Test substrate.** A retired knife is a substrate for new operator training.
- **Failure analysis.** A retired knife from a failure event should be preserved (tagged "FAILURE") for at least 90 days.
- **Hardness test coupon.** A retired knife is a known-grade test piece.
- **Donation to a training institution.** Industrial knife training programs accept retired knives for student practice.

A knife that is truly scrap (e.g. broken in half) goes to the steel recycling bin.

## Common retirement mistakes

1. **Re-grinding past design life.** A 25-re-grind knife becomes a 35-re-grind knife, then a 40-re-grind knife, then chips. The 35th and 40th re-grinds were wasted.
2. **Re-introducing a retired knife because the in-service knives are busy.** This is the most expensive retirement mistake.
3. **Retiring too early.** A 5-re-grind knife with no visible wear does not need to be retired.
4. **Not tagging retired knives.** A retired knife without a red tag is an in-service knife in waiting.

---

## The cost of premature vs late retirement

- **Premature retirement** wastes the remaining life of the knife. Cost: 60 % of knife cost.
- **Late retirement** is the most expensive. A knife that should have been retired at 25 re-grinds and runs to 30 chips on day 26. Cost: 1 day of line downtime + €5,000–€20,000 in scrap.

The right balance is to retire on the four criteria, not on operator gut feeling.

## Field case

**Case: tissue converter, 6 slitting lines.** Customer was retiring knives on operator visual rather than on the four criteria. Service life was inconsistent across lines (8 days to 21 days), and the chip event rate was 8 % per knife. We specified the four criteria, the red-tag protocol, and a retirement log. Service life variance dropped to 14 ± 1 days across all lines. Chip event rate dropped to 1 %. Annual savings: €34,000.

## The retirement SOP

For industrial knife retirement:

1. Inspect every knife on re-grind return.
2. Apply the four retirement criteria.
3. Tag any retired knife with a red marker.
4. Move to the retired knife rack.
5. Log the retirement in the inventory system.
6. Decide re-purpose or scrap.
7. Update the inventory count.

# Hot-Work Tool Steel for Die Casting and Hot Shear

Hot-work tool steel is the family of tool steels designed for tools and dies that operate at elevated temperature (200–600 °C) where thermal fatigue resistance and toughness at operating temperature are the dominant requirements. The defining feature is a moderate alloy content (5 % Cr, 1–2 % Mo, 0.4–0.6 % V) that provides secondary hardening response and temper resistance, combined with high toughness for impact loading.

For industrial cutting tools, hot-work tool steel is the right choice when the cutting edge sees high temperatures from friction or process, AND where toughness matters as much as wear resistance — die-casting dies, hot shear blades, hot piercing punches, extrusion tooling, plastic injection moulds for high-temperature polymers. The 3 grades covered in this guide — AISI H11, H13, and GB 6CrW2Si — span the standard range for industrial hot-work applications.

Selection follows three steps: identify the operating temperature (200–400 °C, 400–600 °C, or 600+ °C), identify the dominant failure mode (thermal fatigue, gross fracture, plastic deformation), and match the grade to the application. H13 is the workhorse die-casting and hot-shear grade; H11 is the lower-V tougher variant; 6CrW2Si is the Chinese GB-standard tungsten-modified alternative.

For per-grade chemistry, heat treatment and cross-reference tables, see the [AISI H11 reference entry](/blog/materials-encyclopedia/h11/), [H13 entry](/blog/materials-encyclopedia/h13/), and [6CrW2Si entry](/blog/materials-encyclopedia/6crw2si/).

## 1. What is hot-work tool steel?

Hot-work tool steel is defined by its ability to retain toughness at elevated temperature. The AISI / UNS standard hot-work grades span HRC 48–54 hardened, with secondary hardening from Mo / V carbide precipitation during tempering and high toughness (Charpy 20–40 J at room temperature) for die-casting and hot-shear duty.

The hot-work family includes:
- **H-series** (5 % Cr) — H11, H12, H13, H21 — the standard hot-work grades
- **W-series variations** — 6CrW2Si (GB), H21 — tungsten-modified for slightly higher hot hardness

The defining feature vs cold-work tool steel is the secondary hardening response: tempering at 510–570 °C precipitates fine Mo₂C / VC carbides that maintain hardness at 500–600 °C operating temperature. Cold-work tool steel loses hardness above 200 °C; hot-work tool steel maintains HRC 42–48 at 600 °C.

The defining feature vs high-speed steel is the lower alloy content — hot-work has 5 % Cr + 1–2 % Mo + 0.4–0.6 % V, while HSS has 4 % Cr + 5–10 % Mo + 1–6 % V. The lower alloy content of hot-work gives lower hardness but higher toughness, which is exactly what die-casting and hot-shear applications need.

## 24. Real-world case: H11 vs H13 for hot piercing punches

A hot piercing operation punches 25 mm diameter holes through 20 mm hot-rolled steel plate at 850 °C. 200 punches/minute. Current punch (H13 at HRC 50): average life 8,000 punches. Failure mode: gross fracture at the punch tip or punch shoulder.

Root cause analysis:
- H13 at HRC 50 has Charpy 20 J at room temperature — marginal for the impact loading of hot piercing
- Punch geometry has a 0.5 mm fillet at the shoulder — stress concentrator
- Operating temperature 850 °C is above the standard hot-work range (typically 600 °C max for H13) — but for short contact times, H13 can survive

Corrective actions:
1. **Switch to H11**: Charpy 30 J vs H13's 20 J. Lower hot hardness (HRC 42 vs HRC 45 at 600 °C) is acceptable because punch duty is impact-dominant, not wear-dominant.
2. **Increase shoulder fillet to 2 mm**: reduces stress concentration.
3. **Reduce punch speed**: 200 → 150 punches/min to lower impact energy per punch.
4. **Apply CrN coating**: 4 µm CrN on the punch tip for wear resistance, leaving the shoulder uncoated for toughness.

**Result**: punch life extended from 8,000 to 18,000 punches (2.25×). Annual punch cost reduction: ~$60,000. ROI: high considering the H11 cost is comparable to H13.

The lesson: hot-work grade selection is not just about hot hardness. For impact-dominant hot-work, toughness (Charpy) is often the limiting factor, favouring H11 over H13. For wear-dominant hot-work, hot hardness is the limiting factor, favouring H13 over H11. The full operating profile — temperature, impact, wear — must be considered.

## 25. Cost-per-cycle and total cost of ownership

Hot-work tool steel selection must consider cost-per-cycle, not just per-blade cost:

**Cost-per-shot for die-casting die** (H13, 100,000 shots die life):
- Die material: $5,000 / 100,000 = $0.05/shot
- Die machining: $2,000 / 100,000 = $0.02/shot
- Die heat-treatment: $1,000 / 100,000 = $0.01/shot
- Die coating (AlCrN): $1,000 / 100,000 = $0.01/shot
- Die maintenance (polishing between runs): $500/cycle × 10 cycles = $5,000 / 100,000 = $0.05/shot
- **Total cost-per-shot**: $0.14

**Compare H13 vs H11 vs H21 (Co-Mo hot-work)**:
- H13: $0.14/shot, die life 100,000 shots
- H11: $0.13/shot, die life 80,000 shots (lower hot hardness)
- H21 (tungsten hot-work): $0.16/shot, die life 130,000 shots (higher hot hardness)

The optimal choice depends on the application. For impact-dominant hot-work, H11's lower cost-per-shot wins. For wear-dominant hot-work, H13 is the sweet spot. For extreme-temperature hot-work (>600 °C), H21 or HSS is justified at higher per-shot cost.

**Hidden costs**: maintenance (polishing, repair), downtime (die change), and quality (rejected parts) are often larger than the direct die cost. A die that lasts 30 % longer but requires 50 % more maintenance may have higher total cost than a shorter-life, lower-maintenance die. Always evaluate on total cost of ownership, not per-blade cost.

## 21. Surface treatments beyond PVD

Beyond PVD coatings (AlCrN, CrN), hot-work tool steel benefits from several other surface treatments:

**Nitriding (gas / plasma / salt-bath)**: diffuses nitrogen into the surface 0.1–0.5 mm, forming a hard nitride layer (~1000 HV). Increases surface hardness to ~HRC 60+ at the surface while keeping the core tough. Standard for hot-work dies and hot shear blades. Plasma nitriding gives better control than gas nitriding.

**Nitrocarburising (ferritic)**: similar to nitriding but adds carbon. Lower temperature process (~570 °C) that avoids dimensional change. Used for ejector pins and slides where minimal distortion is required.

**Oxidation (steam treatment)**: forms a thin Fe₃O₄ layer on the surface that acts as a solid lubricant and corrosion barrier. Used for hot-work dies and punches. The black oxide finish is also corrosion-resistant.

**Boronising**: diffuses boron into the surface, forming very hard FeB / Fe₂B layer (~1500–2000 HV). High temperature (~800–1000 °C) process that can distort the part. Used for severe wear applications but less common for hot-work dies.

**Combination treatments**: many high-performance hot-work parts receive a duplex treatment — nitriding first (for substrate support), then PVD coating (for surface wear resistance). The combined treatment can extend die life 3–5× over single treatments.

## 22. Heat treatment quality control

H13 heat treatment is the most critical step in hot-work die performance. Common quality issues:

**Incorrect austenitising temperature**: 10 °C error shifts final hardness 1–2 HRC. Verify with calibrated thermocouple + pyrometer.

**Quench delay**: delay from austenitising bath to quench should be <30 seconds for air-hardening grades. Longer delays reduce hardness and risk pearlite formation.

**Insufficient tempering**: triple temper is critical for H13. Single or double temper leaves 5–15 % retained austenite, which transforms to martensite during service and causes dimensional change + cracking.

**Wrong tempering temperature**: 510 °C (low) vs 565 °C (high). Low temper gives high hardness but poor temper resistance; high temper gives lower hardness but better toughness.

**Verification methods**:
- Hardness survey: 5+ points, HRC ±1
- Metallographic examination: confirm correct microstructure (tempered martensite + carbides, no untempered martensite, no retained austenite >5 %)
- Fracture toughness K_IC per ASTM E399: H13 should be 25–35 MPa·m^0.5 at HRC 46

For critical dies, the heat-treatment supplier should be ISO 9001 / AS9100 / NADCAP certified for aerospace work.

**Hot piercing punches**: H11 (high impact) or H13 with CrN coating.
## 20. Die-casting die design considerations

Hot-work die design directly affects die life. Key design parameters:

**Cooling channel design**: the most critical factor for die-casting die life. Standard practice:
- Channel diameter 8–12 mm
- Distance from cavity surface 8–12 mm (closer = better cooling, but risk of hot spots and channel-to-cavity breakthrough)
- Conformal cooling (3D-printed channels following cavity geometry) for complex shapes — reduces cycle time 20–40 % and extends die life 2–3×
- Baffles and plugs to direct coolant flow

**Fillets and corners**: all corners must have fillets ≥2 mm (preferably 3–5 mm for hot-work). Sharp corners are stress concentrators that initiate heat-checking cracks.

**Section thickness uniformity**: avoid abrupt changes in section thickness. Uniform thickness allows even cooling and reduces thermal-stress concentration.

**Gate and runner design**: gate location and size affect the thermal load on the cavity surface. Smaller gates give faster cycles but higher thermal stress at the gate area. Best practice: gate area should have additional cooling (baffles or conformal channels).

**Ejector system**: ejector pins should be H13 or H11, hardened to HRC 50, with polished surface finish. DLC coating reduces friction and ejection force.

**Cavity surface finish**: for high-gloss die casting (cosmetic parts), cavity surface polished to Ra 0.1–0.2 µm. For structural parts, Ra 0.4 µm acceptable. Polishing direction should follow the metal flow direction to avoid flow lines.
## 17. Inspection and quality control

Hot-work tool steel QC follows the four-stage protocol with hot-work-specific additions:

**Incoming material inspection**: chemistry per AISI / DIN / JIS / GB standard. Macro-etch test for internal soundness. For ESR / VAR premium material, additional ultrasonic testing for defects.

**Post-machining inspection**: dimensional tolerance per drawing. Surface finish per spec (typically Ra 0.4 µm pre-heat-treatment for die-casting cavities). Visual for surface defects.

**Post-heat-treatment inspection**: hardness HRC ±1 at multiple points. Double or triple temper verified by hardness-after-each-temper pattern (must stabilise by 3rd temper, indicating retained austenite fully decomposed). For critical dies, fracture toughness K_IC per ASTM E399.

**Final inspection**: cavity surface finish per spec (Ra 0.1–0.2 µm polished for high-gloss die casting). Edge geometry for cutting tools. Coating thickness and adhesion test for coated parts (per ASTM C1624).

**Failure analysis protocol**: for failed die-casting dies or hot shear blades, document:
- Shots / cycles completed
- Failure mode (heat-checking, gross fracture, wear, deformation)
- Operating temperature profile (if available)
- Cooling system condition
- Heat-treatment record (temper temperature, hardness profile)

The failure pattern determines the corrective action:
- Heat-checking → improve cooling, drop hardness 1–2 HRC, apply coating
- Gross fracture → larger fillet radius, drop hardness 1–2 HRC, or upgrade to tougher grade (H11)
- Wear → apply coating, upgrade to higher-hot-hardness grade (HSS), or improve lubrication
- Deformation → upgrade to higher-Mo / V grade, reduce operating temperature

## 18. Troubleshooting decision tree

```
Failure symptom           | Likely cause            | First action
--------------------------|------------------------|----------------------
Heat-checking cracks      | Thermal cycling too high| Improve cooling
                          | Temper too low          | Re-temper at 565 °C (double)
                          | No coating              | Apply AlCrN / CrN coating
                          | Operating temp too high | Upgrade to HSS or reduce temp

Gross fracture            | Sharp fillet radius    | Increase fillet to 2–3 mm
                          | Hardness too high       | Drop 2 HRC
                          | Impact too high         | Upgrade to H11 (tougher)

Plastic deformation      | Operating temp too high | Upgrade to HSS
                          | Hardness too low        | Verify heat-treatment
                          | Insufficient section    | Redesign with thicker section

Wear at edge              | Abrasive cutting       | Apply coating
                          | High cutting speed     | Reduce speed or upgrade to HSS
                          | Wrong grade             | Verify grade vs application

                          | Coating too thick      | Reduce thickness
                          | Substrate contamination| Clean substrate pre-coat
```

For complex failures, escalate to a die-engineering specialist. The decision tree covers the 80 % common cases.

## 19. Worked example: H11 vs H13 selection for hot shear

A steel-plate hot shear blade cuts 20 mm hot-rolled mild steel at 600 °C, 200 cuts/hour. Current blade life: H11 at HRC 50, 2 weeks between re-sharpening. Failure mode: edge rounding (hot-collapse) combined with mild heat-checking on the side surfaces.

Grade selection analysis:

| Criterion | H11 | H13 |
|-----------|-----|-----|
| Hot hardness at 600 °C | HRC 42 | HRC 45 |
| Toughness (Charpy) | 25–35 J | 15–25 J |
| Edge stability | Lower | Higher |
| Heat-checking resistance | Lower | Higher |

**Recommendation**: upgrade to H13 at HRC 50 with AlCrN coating. The higher hot hardness gives 30 % longer blade life; the higher V content gives better heat-checking resistance; the AlCrN coating adds another 50 % via friction reduction. Total expected life: 2 weeks × 1.3 × 1.5 = 4 weeks (2× improvement).

The trade-off: H13 has lower toughness than H11, so edge chipping on impact is a slightly higher risk. For this application (steady cutting, no impact), the trade-off favours H13. For intermittent cutting or high-impact loading, H11 would be the safer choice.

Verify with a 1-week trial: install 3 H13 + AlCrN blades, monitor edge wear and chipping. If edge chipping is observed, drop to H11 with the same coating.
## 15. Edge geometry and hone for hot-work blades

Hot-work blade edge geometry differs from cold-work due to the higher operating temperature and the thermal cycling. Key differences:

**Edge radius**: hot shear blades typically run 10–30 µm hone, larger than cold-work blades. The higher temperature and thermal cycling favour a more robust edge over a sharp one.

**Rake angle**: positive rake (5–15°) is standard for hot shear. Higher positive rake reduces cutting force, which reduces heat generation — important for thermal fatigue resistance. For hot piercing punches, zero rake is preferred for rigidity.

**Clearance angle**: 8–15° is standard for hot shear blades. Higher clearance (15–20°) for thermal-cycling applications; lower clearance (5–8°) for high-load applications.

**Secondary bevel**: 25–35° at 0.5–2 mm width is standard for hot shear blades. The secondary bevel dramatically improves chipping resistance under thermal cycling.

**Hone matching rule**: H13 at HRC 50–52 with a 15–25 µm hone is the sweet spot for hot shear blades. H11 at HRC 50 with a 20–30 µm hone is for high-impact applications. 6CrW2Si at HRC 50–52 with a 15–20 µm hone is comparable to H13.

For die-casting ejector pins, the surface finish is critical — ground to Ra 0.4 µm and polished to Ra 0.1–0.2 µm, with 10–20 µm hone at the ejector tip.

## 16. Real-world case: H13 die-casting die thermal-fatigue failure

A aluminium die-casting die for a transmission housing was experiencing thermal-fatigue cracking after ~50,000 shots. Failure mode: heat-checking cracks on the cavity surface near the gate area, propagating to the die surface. Annual die-replacement cost: ~$120,000 (one die, two cavities, machined from H13 block).

Root causes identified:
1. **Insufficient die cooling**: the original cooling channels were 8 mm diameter at 12 mm from the cavity surface. Best practice is 10–12 mm channels at 8–10 mm from the cavity.
2. **H13 grade adequate but temper too low**: original temper was 540 °C (single), giving HRC 48. Best practice is 565 °C (double) giving HRC 46 with better temper resistance.
3. **No coating**: uncoated H13 cavity surface. CrN or AlCrN coating would reduce friction and lower peak cavity temperature.

Corrective actions:
1. **Redesign cooling channels**: 10 mm diameter at 8 mm from cavity surface, with conformal cooling where possible. Verified with thermal simulation.
2. **Re-temper at 565 °C (double)**: drops hardness 2 HRC but improves temper resistance.
3. **Apply AlCrN coating**: 4 µm AlCrN on the cavity surface. Reduces cavity surface temperature by 30–50 °C during the casting cycle.
4. **Implement preventive die-maintenance**: between 25,000 and 50,000 shots, polish the cavity surface to remove early heat-checking cracks before they propagate.

**Result**: die life extended from ~50,000 shots to ~120,000 shots (2.4×). Annual die-replacement cost: $120,000 / 2.4 = $50,000. Total savings: $70,000/year. ROI on the die-engineering effort: 5–10× including the cooling-channel redesign cost.

This case illustrates the typical hot-work die optimisation: cooling + heat-treatment + coating + preventive maintenance. No single intervention is sufficient; the combination multiplies the gains.
## 11. Coatings for hot-work tool steel

PVD coatings are commonly applied to hot-work tool steel for additional wear resistance and thermal barrier effect.

**CrN (Chromium Nitride, 3–6 µm)**: the standard hot-work coating. Hardness ~1800 HV. Excellent thermal stability (oxidation resistance to 700 °C). Silver colour. Provides 2–3× wear life vs uncoated H13.

**AlCrN (Aluminium Chromium Nitride, 3–5 µm)**: harder than CrN (~3200 HV). Excellent thermal stability (to 1100 °C). The premium hot-work coating for die-casting. Blue-grey colour.

**TiAlN (Titanium Aluminium Nitride, 3–5 µm)**: similar to AlCrN, slightly different chemistry. Premium for hot-work.

**DLC (Diamond-Like Carbon, 1–3 µm)**: very low friction. Used for ejector pins and slides where friction is the issue, not wear.

For die-casting dies, the standard practice is to coat the working surface (cavity) with AlCrN, leaving the ejector pins and slides uncoated or DLC-coated. For hot shear blades, CrN or AlCrN coating is standard.

Coating deposition temperature is 350–500 °C. The hot-work substrate is tempered above this temperature before coating (H13 tempered at 540–570 °C). The coating process adds 0.05–0.15 µm to the surface; for sharp blades, re-grind after coating if required.

## 12. Specialty applications

Beyond die-casting and hot shear, hot-work tool steel grades are used in:

**Hot forging dies**: H13 for steel forging, H11 for aluminium forging. The hot hardness maintains the cavity profile at forging temperatures.

**Extrusion tooling**: H13 for aluminium extrusion dies and stems. The high hot hardness resists wear on the die bearing surface.

**Hot piercing punches**: H11 for high-impact piercing of hot steel. The toughness of H11 prevents chipping.

**Plastic injection moulds**: H13 for engineering plastics (PA, POM, PC, PPS, PEEK). The hot hardness maintains mould cavity profile under repeated thermal cycling.

**Forging die inserts**: H13 for steel forging inserts, where the hot hardness and toughness handle the impact and temperature.

**Hot blanking and hot punching**: H13 for blanking and punching of hot-rolled steel. The hot hardness maintains the cutting edge.

## 6. Heat treatment

Hot-work tool steel heat treatment has four stages: preheat, austenitising, quenching, and tempering. The secondary hardening response requires careful tempering control.

**Preheat** at 550–650 °C for 15–30 minutes to equalise temperature. Two-stage preheat (550 °C then 850 °C) is standard for large sections.

**Austenitising** at 1000–1050 °C for H11 / H13 / 6CrW2Si. Hold time 20–30 minutes per inch of section. Higher temperatures dissolve more carbide and raise final hardness, but also raise grain size and risk of retained austenite.

**Quenching** in air (still or positive-pressure gas) for sections through 250 mm. Oil quench for thin sections. Water quench is not recommended — risk of cracking is too high.

**Tempering** at 510–570 °C for 2 hours, double or triple temper. The secondary hardening peak is at 540–570 °C for H13, 540–560 °C for H11, 560–600 °C for 6CrW2Si. Triple temper is critical to fully decompose retained austenite — for die-casting dies that see thermal cycling, incomplete temper means dimensional instability during service.

**Sub-zero treatment** at −80 °C between quench and first temper is recommended for H13 when dimensional stability is critical (precision die-casting dies). Sub-zero should be done within 1 hour of quench.

**Distortion control**: hot-work tool steel has high hardenability. For large die-casting dies, machine with 0.2–0.5 % stock allowance, austenitise, temper, then finish-machine to drawing tolerance. Stress relieve at 600 °C before final machining.

## 7. Selection methodology

Hot-work tool steel selection:

1. **Identify the operating temperature**:
   - 200–400 °C: H11 adequate
   - 400–600 °C: H13
   - 600+ °C: consider HSS (M2 / M42) or carbide

2. **Identify the dominant failure mode**:
   - Thermal fatigue (heat-checking cracks): standard hot-work; verify cooling system
   - Gross fracture (catastrophic break): tougher grade (H11) or redesign with larger fillets
   - Plastic deformation (mushrooming): higher-hot-hardness grade (H13) or reduce operating temperature
   - Wear (edge recession): apply coating (AlCrN, CrN) or upgrade to HSS

3. **Match the grade to the application**:
   - Die-casting die (aluminium, zinc): H13 standard
   - Die-casting die (magnesium): H11 (less reactivity with Mg, lower hot hardness acceptable)
   - Hot shear blades: H13 standard
   - Hot piercing punches: H11 (high impact)
   - Extrusion tooling (aluminium): H13
   - Extrusion tooling (copper): H13 with CrN or AlCrN coating
   - Plastic injection moulds (high-temp polymers): H13
   - Hot forging dies: H13 or H11 depending on impact

4. **Verify with a trial run**: install the candidate die or blade, run 50–100 production cycles, inspect for thermal fatigue cracks, dimensional change, and wear.

5. **Cost-vs-performance trade-off**: H11 and H13 are similar cost. 6CrW2Si is comparable. Upgrade to HSS (M2 / M42) for the hottest applications, but HSS is more expensive.

## 8. Common failure modes

The three most common hot-work blade failures:

**1. Thermal fatigue (heat-checking) cracks.** A die-casting die shows a network of fine cracks on the working surface after several thousand cycles. Visual tell: tight crack pattern perpendicular to heat flow. Fix: improve cooling (die-cooling channels), upgrade to higher-Mo / V grade (H13 → HSS), or apply CrN coating to reduce thermal cycling.

**2. Gross fracture at fillets or corners.** A hot shear blade with sharp corners fractures through the corner. Visual tell: clean break with no wear on the rest of the blade. Fix: increase fillet radius from 0.5 mm to 2–3 mm, drop hardness 2 HRC, or upgrade to H11 (higher toughness).

**3. Plastic deformation (mushrooming).** A long, thin die-casting ejector pin deflects and mushrooms at the tip. Visual tell: rounded tip profile, no chipping. Fix: increase section thickness by 10–15 %, upgrade to higher-hot-hardness grade, or apply nitriding for surface hardening.

## 3. AISI H11

**AISI H11 (UNS T20811, DIN 1.2343, JIS SKD6)** is a 5 % Cr hot-work tool steel with 1.5 % Mo + 0.4 % V. Hardened to HRC 48–53. The lower V content (vs H13) gives slightly less hot hardness but better toughness — H11 is the standard for applications where impact loading is significant.

H11 is used for hot shear blades, die-casting dies for aluminium and magnesium, hot piercing punches, and plastic injection moulds for high-temperature polymers. The Charpy impact of H11 is 25–35 J at room temperature vs H13's 15–25 J — a significant toughness advantage.

H11 selection rule: use H11 when impact loading dominates the failure mode and operating temperature is below 500 °C. Above 500 °C or for the most demanding hot-work duty, upgrade to H13.

## 4. AISI H13

**AISI H13 (UNS T20813, DIN 1.2344, JIS SKD61)** is the workhorse 5 % Cr hot-work tool steel — 1.5 % Mo + 0.9 % V. Hardened to HRC 48–54. The higher V content gives secondary hardening peak at 510–540 °C and hot hardness HRC 45 at 600 °C.

H13 is the most widely used hot-work tool steel globally. Used for:
- Aluminium and zinc die-casting dies (the standard grade worldwide)
- Hot shear blades on hot-rolled steel plate
- Hot extrusion tooling (aluminium, copper, steel)
- Hot forging dies
- Plastic injection moulds for engineering plastics (PA, POM, PC, PPS)
- Die-casting die inserts, cores, ejector pins

H13 selection rule: use H13 for the standard hot-work duty — die casting, hot shear, extrusion. Drop to H11 only when toughness is critical (thin sections, high impact). Above H13, move to HSS (M2, M42) for the hottest applications.

## 5. GB 6CrW2Si

**GB 6CrW2Si (China GB/T 1299 standard)** is a tungsten-modified 5 Cr-type hot-work tool steel. Composition: 0.55–0.65 % C, 5.0–6.0 % Cr, 2.0–2.5 % W, 1.0–1.3 % Si, 0.5–1.0 % Mo. The W provides slightly higher hot hardness than H13 at comparable toughness.

6CrW2Si is widely used in China and Chinese-export tooling as a hot-work alternative to H11/H13. The hot hardness is comparable to H13 at slightly lower cost.

6CrW2Si selection rule: use 6CrW2Si where it is locally available and H13 sourcing is limited. Cross-reference equivalent is approximately between H11 and H13.

The chemistry of hot-work tool steel is dominated by chromium, molybdenum and vanadium.

**Chromium** at 5 % is the baseline. It provides mild corrosion resistance (not passivation-grade), contributes to secondary hardening response, and is the primary carbide former. The M₂₃C₆ and M₇C₃ primary carbides are smaller than in D-series (because less Cr is tied up), giving better toughness.

**Molybdenum** at 1–2 % provides secondary hardening response through Mo₂C precipitation. H11 has 1.5 % Mo, H13 has 1.5 % Mo, 6CrW2Si has similar.

**Vanadium** at 0.4–0.6 % is the key to secondary hardening response. VC precipitates as fine, sub-micron carbides that maintain hardness at operating temperature. H13 has 0.9 % V (high), H11 has 0.4 % V (lower, hence lower hot hardness but higher toughness).

**Tungsten** in 6CrW2Si (~2 % W) provides a slight hot-hardness lift vs the H-series. The W adds ~HRC 1 at operating temperature, useful for slightly hotter applications.

**Molybdenum + Vanadium combination** is what drives the secondary hardening peak at 510–570 °C. The hot hardness of H13 at 600 °C is ~HRC 45 — about HRC 15 below room temperature, but vastly superior to cold-work steel which has lost all practical hardness at 600 °C.

**Chromium + Carbon** drive the hardenability. H13 at 5 % Cr is air-hardening through 250 mm sections, making it ideal for large die-casting dies that need uniform through-thickness hardness.

The trade-off in hot-work tool steel: more V = more hot hardness but less toughness. H13 (0.9 % V) is the workhorse; H11 (0.4 % V) is the tougher lower-V variant. The H21 tungsten-modified grade is the highest-hot-hardness standard hot-work grade (now mostly replaced by HSS for the hottest applications).

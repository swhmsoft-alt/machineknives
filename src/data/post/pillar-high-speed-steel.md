# High-Speed Steel Selection Guide

High-speed steel (HSS) is the family of tool steels designed for cutting at high speeds where the cutting edge reaches 500–650 °C. The defining feature is a combination of high hot hardness (HRC 56–62 at 600 °C) and secondary hardening from Mo / W / V carbide precipitation during tempering. HSS fills the niche between cold-work tool steel (which loses hardness above 200 °C) and cemented carbide (which is brittle and expensive).

For industrial cutting tools, HSS is the right choice when the cutting edge sees high temperatures from friction — high-speed slitting (>500 m/min), dry cutting, abrasive substrates that generate heat, and tooling for machining applications. The 8 grades covered in this guide — M1, M3, M35, M42, M50, T1, T15 and ASP 2060 — span the range from entry-grade Mo-HSS to premium powder-metallurgy grades.

Selection follows three steps: identify the operating temperature (cold work ~200 °C, warm work 200–500 °C, hot work 500–650 °C), identify the dominant failure mode (wear, chipping, hot-collapse), and match the grade to the application. M2 is the workhorse; Co-bearing M35 / M42 extend hot hardness; PM grades like ASP 2060 give 3–5× the wear life at premium cost.

For per-grade chemistry, heat treatment and cross-reference tables, see the [M2 reference entry](/blog/materials-encyclopedia/m2-hss/), [M42 entry](/blog/materials-encyclopedia/m42/), [T1 entry](/blog/materials-encyclopedia/t1/), [T15 entry](/blog/materials-encyclopedia/t15/), [ASP 2060 entry](/blog/materials-encyclopedia/asp2060/), and others linked at the end of this article.

## 1. What is high-speed steel?

High-speed steel is defined by its ability to maintain hardness at elevated temperature — the "red hardness" that gives the family its name. The original tungsten HSS (T1) was patented by Frederick Winslow Taylor and Maunsel White in 1900 for use at cutting speeds 2–3× higher than the carbon tool steels of the day. The Mo-bearing M-series was developed in the 1930s–40s during WWII when W was a strategic material. PM HSS grades (ASP, CPM) became commercial in the 1970s–80s.

HSS achieves hot hardness through secondary hardening — the precipitation of fine Mo₂C / W₂C / VC carbides during tempering at 510–570 °C. These precipitates are sub-micron and resist coarsening at operating temperature. The result: a steel that can be hardened to HRC 63–67 at room temperature and retain HRC 58–62 at 600 °C.

The HSS family used for cutting tools covers:
- **M-series** (Mo-bearing) — M1, M2, M3, M35, M42, M50 — the modern workhorse
- **T-series** (W-bearing) — T1, T15 — the original grades, still used for legacy tooling
- **PM HSS** — ASP 2060, ASP 2030, CPM REX series — premium powder-metallurgy grades

## 25. HSS vs alternative cutting materials

A brief comparison of HSS against alternative cutting materials to clarify when HSS is the right choice:

**HSS vs cold-work tool steel (D-series)**: HSS has higher hot hardness (HRC 56–63 at 600 °C vs D2's HRC loss above 200 °C). Cold-work steel is cheaper. Choose cold-work for slow, low-temperature cutting; choose HSS for high-speed or high-temperature cutting.

**HSS vs martensitic stainless (440C)**: 440C has corrosion resistance (food, marine); HSS has higher hot hardness. Choose stainless for corrosion-sensitive applications; choose HSS for high-speed cutting in non-corrosive environments.

**HSS vs hot-work tool steel (H13)**: H13 retains hardness at 600 °C (secondary hardening) but less than HSS. H13 has much higher toughness for impact loading. Choose H13 for die-casting and hot shear; choose HSS for high-speed cutting.

**HSS vs carbide (YG / PCD)**: Carbide has much higher hardness (HRA 90 vs HRC 65) but is brittle. Choose carbide for ultra-premium wear life on abrasive substrates; choose HSS for toughness-critical applications or where tooling cost is a concern.

**HSS vs ceramic (Si₃N₄, Al₂O₃)**: Ceramics are used for very high-speed machining (>2,000 m/min) of cast iron or hardened steel. Industrial slitter blades rarely use ceramic due to brittleness and cost.

The decision matrix for industrial cutting tools is approximately:
- <200 °C, low speed, abrasive → D-series or HSS
- 200–500 °C, medium speed → M2 / M35
- 500–650 °C, high speed, abrasive → M42 / ASP 2060
- Corrosion-sensitive → 440C stainless
- Ultra-premium abrasive wear → Carbide (YG6, YG8)
- Impact-dominant, hot → H13 hot-work steel

HSS occupies the high-temperature / high-speed niche between cold-work and carbide.
## 23. Economics of HSS selection

The cost-vs-performance trade-off in HSS blade selection follows a clear pattern:

**Baseline**: M2 uncoated at $15/kg finished. Service life 5 days at 600 m/min. Annual blade cost (for a typical 200-blade line) = $30,000.

**Step 1 — Add AlCrN coating** (M2 + AlCrN at $25/kg, 1.8× cost): service life extends to 10 days (2× wear life). Annual cost = $33,750 (1.13×). ROI: 50 % wear-life extension for 13 % cost increase.

**Step 2 — Upgrade to M42** (M42 + AlCrN at $40/kg, 2.7× cost): service life extends to 20 days (4× wear life). Annual cost = $54,000 (1.8×). ROI: 300 % wear-life extension for 80 % cost increase.

**Step 3 — Upgrade to ASP 2060 with ta-C** (at $100/kg, 6.7× cost): service life extends to 60 days (12× wear life). Annual cost = $200,000 (6.7×). ROI: 1100 % wear-life extension for 570 % cost increase.

**Diminishing returns**: each step gives a larger absolute wear-life gain but a smaller ROI ratio. For most production lines, Step 2 (M42 + AlCrN) is the sweet spot — the 4× wear life justifies the 1.8× cost without the complexity of PM-grade sourcing.

For ultra-premium applications (high-speed cutting >1,500 m/min on abrasive substrates), Step 3 (ASP 2060 + ta-C) is justified. For low-speed or low-volume applications, Step 1 (M2 + AlCrN) is sufficient.

**Hidden cost factor**: each upgrade typically requires grinding-wheel changes (AlCrN / ta-C are harder than M2 substrate and require different wheel specifications), inspection protocol upgrades, and operator training. Total "cost of ownership" includes these items, not just blade material cost.

## 24. Final summary

The high-speed steel family — M-series (M1, M2, M3, M35, M42, M50), T-series (T1, T15), and PM grades (ASP 2060) — covers the range from entry-grade Mo-HSS at HRC 63–65 to premium powder-metallurgy grades at HRC 67–69 with hot hardness HRC 56–63 at 600 °C. The defining metallurgical feature is secondary hardening from Mo / W / V carbide precipitation during tempering. Selection follows three steps: identify the operating temperature and cutting speed, identify the substrate abrasion, and match the grade to the application. M2 is the workhorse; M35 / M42 extend hot hardness for high-speed applications; ASP 2060 gives 3–5× the wear life of M42 at premium cost. Coatings (AlCrN, ta-C) extend wear life 2–5× at modest cost premium and are standard for high-speed cutting.

## 21. Inspection and quality control

HSS blade QC follows the same four-stage protocol as cold-work tool steel (see [cold-work pillar §18](/blog/selection-guide/pillar-cold-work-tool-steel/)) with HSS-specific additions:

**Incoming material inspection**: chemistry vs ASTM A600 or ISO 4957 specification. Macro-etch test on sample pieces to verify internal soundness. Carbide distribution rating for PM grades (must be uniform, no clusters >20 µm).

**Post-machining inspection**: dimensional tolerance (±0.01 mm for slitter blades, ±0.05 mm for form tools). Surface finish per drawing (typically Ra 0.4 µm pre-heat-treatment, Ra 0.2 µm post-grinding). Visual for surface defects (grinding cracks, decarburisation).

**Post-heat-treatment inspection**: hardness HRC ±1 at multiple points. Triple-temper verified by hardness-after-each-temper pattern (must be stable across all three tempers, indicating retained austenite fully decomposed). For critical applications, K_IC fracture toughness testing per ASTM E399.

**Final inspection**: edge geometry (radius, rake, clearance) per drawing. Visual for grind burns (oxidation discoloration at edge). For coated blades, coating thickness measurement (typically XRF or ball-crater test) and adhesion test (scratch test per ASTM C1624).

**Failure analysis protocol**: save failed blades, document:
- Position in line, substrate processed, hours of service, failure mode (chipping, hot-collapse, wear, deformation)
- Photos at 1×, 10×, and 100× magnification
- Hardness at failure site (verify heat-treatment was within spec)
- Coating condition (if coated) — coating intact, partial spallation, full spallation

The failure mode determines the corrective action:
- Chipping → drop hardness or add hone
- Hot-collapse → upgrade to higher-Co grade or reduce cutting speed
- Uniform wear → upgrade to higher-V grade or add coating
- Grinding cracks → re-grind with proper parameters
- Coating spallation → re-coat or upgrade to better coating

## 22. Troubleshooting decision tree

A quick reference for diagnosing HSS blade failures in the field:

```
Failure symptom | Likely cause | First action
----------------|--------------|-------------
Edge chipping   | Impact too high for current hardness | Drop 2 HRC, or add 10–20 µm hone
                | Hone too sharp | Increase hone by 5 µm
                | Hardness too high | Drop 2–4 HRC
                | Substrate contamination | Audit upstream material flow

Hot-collapse    | Cutting speed too high | Reduce speed, or upgrade M2 → M35 → M42
                | No coating | Apply AlCrN
                | Substrate underperforming | Upgrade to higher-Co grade
                | Insufficient coolant | Audit coolant flow

Uniform wear    | Abrasive substrate | Upgrade to M42, or apply coating
                | Coating worn through | Re-coat, or upgrade substrate
                | Wrong grade for substrate | Audit grade vs substrate hardness

Gross fracture  | Grinding cracks | Re-grind with proper parameters, or scrap
                | Heat-treat defect | Audit heat-treat records, or scrap
                | Design issue (sharp corner) | Add fillet, redesign

Coating spall   | Substrate under-cooked | Re-temper above coating deposition temp
                | Coating too thick | Reduce coating thickness
                | Substrate contamination | Clean substrate pre-coat
                | Coating process defect | Return to coater
```

For complex failures, escalate to a blade failure analyst. The decision tree covers the 80 % common cases; the remaining 20 % require metallurgy expertise.
## 18. HSS for woodworking and non-metal cutting

While HSS is most associated with metal cutting, it has important applications in woodworking and non-metal cutting:

**Wood planer and jointer knives**: M2 or M42 HSS knives for woodworking planers, jointers, and moulders. The hot hardness maintains edge profile at the high surface speeds (~3,000 m/min) typical of woodworking machinery. HSS knives can be re-sharpened 10–20 times vs 3–5 times for tool steel knives, dramatically extending tool life.

**Fibre cement cutting**: HSS blades (typically M2 or M42 with AlCrN coating) for cutting fibre-cement board, cement board, and similar abrasive construction materials. The hot hardness handles the heat from cutting dense cement composites.

**Plastic granulator blades**: HSS is sometimes used for plastic granulator rotors where the substrate generates significant friction heat. M2 or M35 with AlCrN coating is common.

**Paper and pulp cutting**: M2 HSS slitter blades for paper mills. The hot hardness maintains edge profile at the high surface speeds of paper-making machinery. Stainless or coated HSS preferred for wet-end applications.

**Carbon fibre and composites**: HSS is generally not the first choice for carbon fibre reinforced polymer (CFRP) cutting — diamond or PCD tooling is preferred. But for occasional CFRP cutting, M42 or ASP 2060 with diamond-like coating works.

## 19. Standards & quality

HSS is covered by multiple international standards. Key standards:

**ASTM A600** — Standard specification for high-speed tool steel. Defines chemistry, hardness, and macrostructure requirements for wrought HSS.

**ISO 4957** — Tool steels. Includes HSS grades (HS 6-5-2 = M2, HS 2-9-1-8 = M42, HS 18-0-1 = T1, etc.). European / international standard.

**JIS G4403** — High-speed tool steel. Japanese standard.

**DIN EN ISO 4957** — European adoption of ISO 4957.

**AMS 6491** — Aerospace material specification for M50 HSS.

For PM HSS, the standards are more limited. UDDEHOLM ASP grades are proprietary; Crucible CPM grades are proprietary. Cross-references to ISO / DIN equivalents are approximate.

**Mill specifications for HSS** should include:
- AISI / UNS grade designation
- Heat-treatment condition (annealed for machining, or pre-hardened to spec)
- Chemistry with mill certificate
- Hardness range
- Macro-etch test result (for internal defects)
- Carbide distribution rating (for PM grades)
- Surface finish (hot-rolled, cold-drawn, ground)

For aerospace / medical applications, additional requirements:
- ESR (electroslag remelting) or VAR (vacuum arc remelting) for clean steel
- Ultrasonic testing for internal defects
- Fracture toughness testing (K_IC) for critical applications

## 16. Specialty applications

Beyond slitter blades, HSS grades are widely used in:

**Cutting tools for machining**: M2 is the workhorse for drills, taps, end mills, and milling cutters. M42 is the premium grade for hard machining (HRC 50+ workpiece). ASP 2060 is the ultra-premium for the hardest workpieces.

**Broaches**: M2 or M42 for standard broaches. The high hot hardness of HSS maintains the cutting edge profile during the long, continuous cuts of broaching.

**Form tools**: T15 or M3 for form tools (gear cutters, thread rolls). The high V content resists wear on the form profile.

**Cold header dies**: M2 or M42 for cold-heading dies that punch and form steel fasteners at high speed. The hot hardness of HSS maintains the cutting edge through the heat generated by deformation.

**Saws**: M2 or M42 for metal-cutting circular saws and bandsaws. The high hot hardness maintains the tooth profile at high cutting speeds.

**Razor blades**: M2 historically, now mostly stainless steel for corrosion resistance. HSS still used for industrial razor blades that need edge retention over corrosion.

**Engraving tools**: M2 or M42 for engraving cutters used in tool-and-die shops and jewellery making.

## 17. Real-world case: M2 → M42 upgrade on tissue slitter

A tissue-converting line was experiencing M2 slitter blade service life of 5 days at 600 m/min. Annual blade cost: ~$80,000. Failure mode: hot-collapse — the edge rounded over, no chipping, blades were running hot.

The upgrade sequence:

1. **Diagnose root cause**: 600 m/min with no coating = hot edge. M2 loses HRC 5–6 between room temperature and 600 °C, dropping from HRC 64 to ~HRC 58. The edge deforms plastically.

2. **Upgrade to M42**: M42 holds HRC 60–62 at 600 °C vs M2's HRC 56. Same room-temperature hardness (HRC 65–68) but better at operating temperature.

3. **Apply AlCrN coating**: 3 µm AlCrN reduces friction coefficient by 30 %, lowering cutting-edge temperature by 50–80 °C. Coating hardness 3200 HV adds wear resistance.

4. **Verify with a 1-week wear test**: install 5 M42+AlCrN blades, run 7 days, measure edge wear (micrometer) and document any chipping. Result: service life extended to 14 days.

5. **Optional: add ta-C topcoat**: for further extension, ta-C (1 µm) on top of AlCrN gives ~30 % additional wear life at +20 % cost.

**Result**: blade service life extended from 5 to 14 days (2.8×). Annual cost: $80,000 / 2.8 = $28,600. Total savings: $51,400/year. ROI on the upgrade effort: 50×.

The key insight: hot-collapse failure is the symptom of M2 being outclassed at high cutting speed. The fix is not more frequent re-sharpening — it is upgrading to a grade with better hot hardness (M42) and reducing friction (AlCrN coating).
## 15. Coatings for HSS

PVD coatings are standard for HSS cutting tools. The substrate hot hardness of HSS (HRC 56+ at 600 °C) supports the coating at operating temperature — a critical advantage over cold-work tool steel which would burn through the coating deposition process.

**TiN (Titanium Nitride, 2–4 µm)**: the standard gold-coloured HSS coating. Hardness ~2300 HV. Provides ~2× wear life vs uncoated HSS at low cost. Suitable for general-purpose cutting at moderate speeds.

**TiCN (Titanium Carbonitride, 2–4 µm)**: harder than TiN (~3000 HV). Better for abrasive cutting. Grey-purple colour.

**AlCrN (Aluminium Chromium Nitride, 2–4 µm)**: the most versatile HSS coating. Hardness ~3200 HV. Excellent thermal stability (oxidation resistance to 1100 °C). Standard for high-speed cutting. Blue-grey colour.

**DLC (Diamond-Like Carbon, 1–3 µm)**: very low coefficient of friction (~0.1). Reduces adhesion and built-up edge on sticky substrates (aluminium, plastic). Limited to ~350 °C operating temperature.

**ta-C (tetrahedral amorphous Carbon, 1–3 µm)**: hardest coating available (~5000 HV). Premium solution for abrasive cutting at high speed. Used with M42 or ASP 2060 for the most aggressive cutting applications. Limited to ~500 °C operating temperature.

**Coating selection rule**:
- General cutting: AlCrN (best value)
- Abrasive cutting: AlCrN (3 µm) or AlCrN + DLC duplex
- High-speed (1,000+ m/min): ta-C on M42 or ASP 2060
- Sticky substrates (Al, plastic): DLC

Coating deposition temperature is 350–500 °C. Tempering above this temperature before coating is critical — otherwise the coating process itself tempers the substrate and shifts final hardness. The coating process adds 0.05–0.15 µm to the edge radius; for sharp slitter blades, re-grind after coating if edge radius <3 µm is required.

See the [coatings comparison table](/blog/coatings-comparison/) for detailed chemistry, hardness, thickness and deposition-temperature limits of each coating type.
## 14. Edge geometry and hone for HSS

HSS edge geometry follows the same principles as cold-work tool steel (see the [cold-work pillar §17](/blog/selection-guide/pillar-cold-work-tool-steel/)) but with critical differences for high-speed and high-temperature applications:

**Edge radius**: HSS slitter blades for tissue/film typically run 2–10 µm hone (vs 5–15 µm for cold-work). The higher hardness allows sharper edges without chipping. For abrasive substrates (mineral-filled polymer, glass fibre), 10–20 µm hone is needed.

**Rake angle**: positive rake (10–20°) is standard for HSS slitter blades. Higher positive rake reduces cutting force and heat generation — important for hot applications. Zero or negative rake is for hard, brittle substrates.

**Clearance angle**: 8–12° is standard for HSS. Higher clearance (15–20°) is for heat-sensitive applications; lower clearance (5–8°) is for thin, fragile substrates.

**Secondary bevel**: 25–35° at 0.05–0.2 mm width is standard for high-speed HSS slitter blades. The secondary bevel dramatically improves chipping resistance without sacrificing cut quality.

**Hone matching rule**: M2 at HRC 65 with a 5 µm hone is the sweet spot for most tissue / film slitting. M42 at HRC 68 with a 10 µm hone is for abrasive substrates. ASP 2060 with a 5–10 µm hone and ta-C coating is the premium configuration.

## 7. Selection methodology

A pragmatic selection methodology for high-speed tool steel in industrial blades:

1. **Identify the operating temperature**:
   - <200 °C (cool, slow cutting): cold-work D-series adequate; HSS not required
   - 200–500 °C (warm, moderate cutting): M2 baseline
   - 500–600 °C (hot, fast cutting): M35 or M42
   - 600–650 °C (very hot, high-speed cutting): M42, ASP 2060, or consider carbide

2. **Identify the cutting speed**:
   - <300 m/min: M2 adequate
   - 300–800 m/min: M2 with AlCrN coating; or M35
   - 800–1,200 m/min: M42; or M35 with ta-C coating
   - >1,200 m/min: M42 with ta-C coating; or ASP 2060

3. **Identify the substrate abrasion**:
   - Low abrasion (paper, tissue, film): M2
   - Medium abrasion (filled polymers, abrasive film): M35 or M42
   - High abrasion (mineral-filled polymer, glass fibre, ceramic): M42 with coating, or ASP 2060

4. **Check toughness requirement**:
   - Thin sections (<2 mm), impact loading: M2 or M35 (avoid M42, lower toughness)
   - Standard sections (2–10 mm): any M-series
   - Heavy sections (>10 mm): ASP 2060 (higher hot hardness retained through thickness)

5. **Check coating compatibility**:
   - Most HSS grades accept PVD coatings (TiN, TiCN, AlCrN, DLC, ta-C)
   - ta-C coating (4–7 µm) on M42 or ASP 2060 is the premium solution for abrasive cutting
   - Coating deposition temperature 350–500 °C requires tempering above this temperature before coating

6. **Cost-vs-performance trade-off**: M2 at ~$10–20/kg is the benchmark. M42 at $30–50/kg is the standard premium. ASP 2060 at $50–100/kg is the high-end. The wear-life premium at 2–5× can justify the cost premium at high-volume production.

7. **Match the grade to the application**:
   - General-purpose slitter (<300 m/min): M2
   - High-speed slitter (300–800 m/min): M2 + AlCrN coating, or M35
   - Premium slitter (800–1,200 m/min): M42 + ta-C coating
   - Ultra-premium slitter (>1,200 m/min, abrasive): ASP 2060 + ta-C
   - Drill / tap / milling cutter: M2
   - Form tool (precision): T15 or M3
   - Broach (heavy duty): M2 or M42

## 9. Common failure modes

The four most common HSS blade failures in production:

**1. Edge chipping on impact loading.** An M42 slitter blade at HRC 68 chatters on a coated-paper line where the coating has variable thickness. Visual tell: 0.1–2 mm edge fragments missing, often accompanied by wire-edge. Fix: drop hardness 2 HRC, or move to M35 (higher toughness), or add 10–20 µm hone.

**2. Hot-collapse at high cutting speed.** A M2 blade running at 1,000 m/min loses hardness and deforms plastically at the edge. Visual tell: rounded edge profile, no chipping, edge is dull, blade runs hot. Fix: upgrade to M42 or M35 (higher Co → higher hot hardness), or reduce cutting speed, or apply ta-C coating to reduce friction heating.

**3. Gross fracture from grinding cracks.** An M2 blade with visible grind burns (blue / straw discoloration at the edge) fractures through the burn zone after a few weeks of service. Visual tell: clean break with visible oxidation at the fracture surface. Fix: re-grind with sharp wheels and abundant coolant; or scrap the burned blade.

**4. Catastrophic wear on abrasive substrates.** An M2 blade on glass-fibre-filled polymer wears to a knife edge within 2 weeks. Visual tell: uniform edge recession, no chipping. Fix: upgrade to M42 with ta-C coating, or to ASP 2060.

A useful diagnostic is to inspect a worn HSS blade under 10–30× magnification before scrapping it. The wear pattern tells the story: edge chipping → impact problem; hot-collapse → temperature problem; grinding cracks → manufacturing defect; uniform wear → abrasive substrate problem.
## 5. Powder-metallurgy HSS

Powder-metallurgy (PM) HSS grades are produced by gas-atomising the molten steel into fine powder, then hot-isostatically pressing (hipping) the powder into billets. The PM process distributes the carbides uniformly throughout the matrix, eliminating the large primary carbides of cast/wrought HSS that limit grindability and toughness.

The benefits of PM HSS:
- **Uniform carbide distribution** — no large primary carbides to chip out during grinding
- **Higher alloy content possible** — V can go to 6–10 % (vs 2–3 % in wrought HSS) without grinding problems
- **Improved toughness** — at the same hardness, PM HSS has 2–3× the toughness of wrought HSS
- **Better grindability** — critical for high-V grades where wrought HSS would burn through grinding wheels

**ASP 2060 (UDDEHOLM / Voestalpine)** is the premium PM HSS covered in this guide — 4 % Cr, 7 % Mo, 6.5 % W, 6.5 % V, 10.5 % Co. Hardened to HRC 67–69. The highest alloy content of any standard PM HSS in this grade class. Used for high-speed slitter blades on abrasive paper / film and cutting tools where M42 wear life is insufficient. Cost: 5–10× the price of M2.

Other PM HSS grades (not covered in detail here):
- **ASP 2030** — lower V, lower Co than 2060; intermediate cost/performance
- **CPM REX M4** (Crucible) — equivalent to ASP 2060 from US supplier
- **CPM REX T15** — equivalent to wrought T15 in PM form
- **S390** (Bohler) — high-V, high-Co PM grade

PM HSS selection rule: use ASP 2060 (or equivalent PM grade) when M42 / T15 wear life is insufficient and the 5–10× cost premium is justified by longer service life. For slitter blades on highly abrasive substrates, ASP 2060 with ta-C coating is the standard premium solution.

## 6. Heat treatment

HSS heat treatment has four stages: preheat, austenitising, quenching, and tempering. Each step is critical for final properties.

**Preheat** at 550–600 °C for 10–20 minutes to equalise temperature and reduce thermal shock on insertion to the austenitising bath. Two-stage preheat (450 °C then 850 °C) is standard for high-alloy HSS like M42 and ASP 2060.

**Austenitising** at 1180–1230 °C (M-series) or 1240–1280 °C (T-series, ASP 2060). Hold time 2–5 minutes per inch of section — much shorter than cold-work grades because the goal is to dissolve carbides without grain growth. Higher temperatures dissolve more V / Mo / W carbides and raise final hardness, but also raise retained austenite that requires more tempering cycles.

**Quenching** in oil (50–80 °C), salt bath (550–600 °C for martempering), or positive-pressure gas. The martempering bath at 550–600 °C followed by air cooling to room temperature reduces distortion for long, thin tools like slitter blades.

**Tempering** is the critical step for HSS hot hardness. The secondary-hardening peak is at 510–570 °C for most HSS grades. Standard practice: triple temper at 540–570 °C for 2 hours each. The triple temper is necessary to fully decompose retained austenite (which forms during the quench and would otherwise destabilise the dimensions over time).

For ASP 2060: austenitise at 1100–1180 °C, triple temper at 540–570 °C. The lower austenitising temperature (vs wrought HSS) is because the uniform PM microstructure dissolves carbides more readily.

**Sub-zero treatment** at −196 °C (liquid nitrogen) between quench and first temper is recommended for M42, M3, T15, ASP 2060 to maximise martensite formation before the first temper. Done within 1 hour of quench.

**Distortion control**: HSS has high hardenability and tolerates aggressive quenching, but distortion is still significant. For slitter blades, rough-grind with 0.1–0.2 mm stock, austenitise, then finish-grind to final tolerance. Stress-relieve at 600 °C before final machining.
## 3. The M-series: Mo-bearing high-speed steel

The M-series (M1, M2, M3, M35, M42, M50) is the modern workhorse HSS family. With 4–10 % Mo and 1–2 % V, the M-series provides hot hardness and wear resistance at lower cost than the T-series (because Mo is cheaper than W per unit of effect).

**AISI M1 (UNS T11301)** is the entry-grade Mo-HSS — 8.5 % Mo, 1 % V. Hardened to HRC 63–65. The first commercial Mo-HSS, historically significant but now superseded by M2 for most applications. Used for drills, taps, and entry-grade slitter blades where M2 cost is overkill.

**AISI M2 (UNS T11302)** is the workhorse of the M-series and the most widely used HSS grade globally — 5 % Mo, 6 % W, 2 % V. Hardened to HRC 63–65. Used for slitter blades, drills, taps, milling cutters, broaches across all converting and metalworking applications. M2 has the lowest cost per unit of hot hardness of any HSS grade and is the default choice for general-purpose high-speed cutting.

**AISI M3 (UNS T11313)** is the high-V HSS — available in Class 1 (1.0 % C, 2.75 % V) and Class 2 (1.2 % C, 3.25 % V). Hardened to HRC 64–66. The high V gives the highest abrasive wear resistance in the standard M-series, at the cost of grindability.

**AISI M35 (UNS T11335)** is the 5 % Co modification of M2 — cobalt raises the hot hardness from HRC 56 (M2 at 600 °C) to HRC 60 (M35 at 600 °C). Hardened to HRC 63–66. Used for high-speed slitter blades (>800 m/min on tissue / film), dry cutting, and applications where M2 hot hardness is marginal.

**AISI M42 (UNS T11342)** is the 8 % Co super-HSS — the highest hot hardness of any standard HSS, HRC 60–62 at 600 °C. Hardened to HRC 65–69. Wear life is 1.5–2× M2 on abrasive substrates, at the cost of toughness (more Co means higher carbide volume, lower impact resistance). Used for high-speed slitter blades (>1,200 m/min), high-temperature cutting with ta-C coating, and premium tooling applications.

**AISI M50 (UNS T11350, AMS 6491)** is a Cr-V-Mo HSS for aerospace bearings — HRC 64–66 with good dimensional stability. Not typically used for cutting tools but specified where tight dimensional tolerance is required post-heat-treatment.

M-series selection rule: start with M2 as the default. Move to M35 for high-speed dry cutting (>800 m/min). Move to M42 for the most abrasive cutting (>1,200 m/min). Move to M3 if M2 wear life is insufficient and grindability is not a constraint. Move to ASP 2060 if M42 wear life is still insufficient and the 5–10× cost premium is justified.

## 4. The T-series: W-bearing high-speed steel

The T-series (T1, T15) is the original tungsten HSS family. While superseded by the T-series for general production, the W-V-Co combination in some T-series grades gives specific property advantages that keep them in use.

**AISI T1 (UNS T12001)** is the original 18 % W tungsten HSS — the historical baseline of the entire HSS family. Hardened to HRC 63–65. Used for legacy tooling designs and applications where the deep-hardening response of T1 is preferred over M2.

**AISI T15 (UNS T12015)** is the Co-W-V super-HSS — 12 % W, 5 % V, 5 % Co. Hardened to HRC 66–68. The combination of high W + high V gives the highest abrasive wear resistance of any standard (non-PM) HSS grade. Used for precision form tools, taps, broaches, and cutting tools on the most abrasive substrates where T1 / M2 wear life is insufficient.

T-series selection rule: use T1 only for legacy tooling or when M-series is unavailable. Use T15 for the most abrasive cutting where PM grades are over-budget. The T-series is generally more expensive than the M-series at equivalent hot hardness.
The M-series is preferred over T-series in modern production because Mo is ~3× less expensive than W (per unit of hot-hardness effect), so M-series grades are cheaper at equivalent performance. The T-series remains in use for legacy tooling designs and for grades like T15 where the W-V-Co combination gives specific property advantages.

## 2. Chemistry and secondary hardening

The chemistry of HSS is dominated by four elements: carbon, molybdenum (or tungsten), vanadium, and (for premium grades) cobalt.

**Carbon** drives the carbide volume fraction. HSS contains 0.7–1.5 % C, much higher than cold-work steel. The carbon combines with Mo, W, V to form the hard carbides that provide both primary wear resistance and secondary hardening.

**Molybdenum** (in M-series) provides hot hardness through Mo₂C precipitation during tempering. M2 has 5 % Mo, M42 has 9–10 % Mo. The Mo is partly in primary carbides (M₆C) and partly in solution that precipitates during tempering.

**Tungsten** (in T-series) provides hot hardness through W₂C precipitation. T1 has 18 % W, T15 has 12 % W. The higher W content makes T-series more expensive than M-series.

**Vanadium** is the hardest carbide former — VC at ~2700 HV drives wear resistance in HSS. M2 has 2 % V, M3 has up to 3.25 % V, ASP 2060 has 6.5 % V. Higher V means higher wear resistance but also lower grindability.

**Cobalt** boosts hot hardness by raising the tempering temperature at which the secondary-hardening carbides coarsen. M35 has 5 % Co, M42 has 8 % Co, ASP 2060 has 10.5 % Co. Co-bearing grades retain hardness at higher operating temperatures — M42 holds HRC 60 at 600 °C vs M2's HRC 56.

**Chromium** (3.5–4.5 %) provides mild corrosion resistance and contributes to hardenability. Not the dominant element in HSS.

The chemistry-vs-property mapping is direct: Mo / W gives hot hardness; V gives wear resistance; Co gives red-hardness retention; Cr gives corrosion resistance. The PM grades use powder metallurgy to distribute the carbides more uniformly, eliminating the large primary carbides of cast/wrought HSS that limit grindability and toughness.

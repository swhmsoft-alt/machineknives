
## 3. Comparing D2, A2, O1, SKD11 and DC53 — chemistry, hardness, grindability

Five grades cover the vast majority of cold-work slitter specifications. The table below compares nominal chemistry (weight percent), achievable working hardness, primary carbide volume fraction and relative grindability cost. Grindability is rated relative to O1 (1.0×); higher numbers mean slower grinding and faster wheel wear.

| Grade | C | Cr | Mo | V | Working HRC | Carbide vol % | Grindability |
|-------|-----|-----|-----|-----|-------------|----------------|--------------|
| AISI O1 | 0.90 | 0.50 | 0.25 | 0.20 | 58–62 | ~3 | 1.0× (best) |
| AISI A2 | 1.00 | 5.00 | 1.00 | 0.30 | 58–62 | ~6 | 1.3× |
| AISI D2 | 1.50 | 12.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 58–62 | ~12 | 3.5× |
| JIS SKD11 | 1.50 | 11.50 | 1.00 | 0.40 | 58–62 | ~11 | 2.5× |
| Daido DC53 | 1.00 | 8.00 | 2.00 | 0.30 | 60–64 | ~5 | 1.5× |

AISI O1 remains in service for short-run tooling, prototype tooling and low-volume blade production where the simplicity of oil quenching and the ease of grinding outweigh the lower wear life. The 0.9 % carbon and modest alloy content mean O1 can be heat-treated in almost any shop furnace without atmosphere control, which is why it still appears in small-batch and field-repair specifications.

AISI A2 is the most versatile of the five. Five percent chromium gives air-hardening through 25 mm sections — enough for nearly every slitter-blade geometry — and the modest 1 % Mo delivers a useful secondary-hardening response at tempering temperatures of 200–540 °C. Working hardness typically lands at 58–62 HRC depending on the temper schedule. A2 is the grade of choice when the wear mode is mixed (some abrasion, some impact), when distortion must be controlled, and when the blade shop's grinding equipment is conventional aluminium-oxide rather than CBN.

AISI D2 was covered above. Its combination of 12 % Cr, 1 % Mo and 1 % V delivers the highest wear life of the standard AISI grades, at the cost of grinding difficulty.

JIS SKD11 is the Japanese equivalent of D2 but with a deliberately lower vanadium specification (0.4 % vs 1.0 %). The lower vanadium gives slightly better grindability than D2 at the cost of a small reduction in primary MC carbide volume. SKD11 is widely available from Japanese and Korean mills (Daido, Hitachi, Bohler Japan) and is the default specification in much of the Asian blade-supply chain. Functionally, an SKD11 blade and a D2 blade at equivalent hardness are interchangeable for most slitter service.

Daido DC53 is the proprietary refinement of SKD11. The chemistry drops carbon to 1.0 % (matching A2) but raises chromium to 8 % and molybdenum to 2 %. The lower carbon reduces primary carbide volume to roughly 5 %, but the higher molybdenum refines the carbide distribution and lifts toughness to roughly twice that of SKD11 at equivalent hardness. DC53 has become the default upgrade for high-impact, chipping-prone slitter applications — particularly in paper and film lines where D2 occasionally chips at the edge and DC53 does not. The trade-off is wear life: DC53 at 62 HRC runs about 60–70 % of the wear life of D2 at 60 HRC, which is why the upgrade to DC53 is justified only when the failure mode is chipping, not gradual wear.
